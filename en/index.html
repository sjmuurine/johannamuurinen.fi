Politics

I am running for Parliament.

I am running because research does not find its way into decisions.

Why I am running

We hold the keys to solving many problems, but they are not being used.

I went to university at 28 because I wanted to become a researcher and try to save the world. Quite soon I realised that science alone will not save the world, and that a research result does not turn into a political decision by itself.

I often describe the situation with a metaphor. Humanity needs chairs, and researchers make wooden parts that fit them. The parts would fit together perfectly, but nobody assembles any chairs from them. In other words, we hold the keys to solving many problems, but they are not being used.

I was drawn into politics by a fellow student who was active in local politics in Sipoo. My friend asked me whether I knew that as a local decision-maker I could sit at the table where building permits that cut through green corridors are decided. Such a decision weakens the biodiversity that protects our health. Construction that fragments natural habitats affects, for example, how the habitats of tick-carrying deer and people overlap. When I looked into it, I found that a surprising number of questions about sustainable development and biodiversity loss are decided in municipalities. I stood for election even though I had only recently moved to Sipoo and only a handful of people knew me. I became the Greens' first deputy councillor. On the building and environment committee I now bring research-based knowledge into decisions, for example on scattered construction outside planned areas.

Now I want to bring research-based knowledge into decision-making more widely. I refuse to accept that political debate revolves around hardship and painful decisions, as if there were no chance of anything better. As a researcher I am used to looking for solutions. Scientific research often starts from a problem facing humanity that it tries to solve. In the EU-funded LEX4BIO project, for example, I studied the microbiological risks of recycled fertilisers, because fear of those risks is one reason why people are wary of using them. The results were reassuring: recycled fertilisers do not spread antibiotic resistance in agricultural ecosystems any more than ordinary farmyard manure. Recycled fertilisers would reduce farming's dependence on fossil energy and mineral fertilisers and promote the circular economy. So the solutions exist, as long as we put them to use.

For solutions to keep emerging in the future, we need research and product development, and wise support for both. Securing Finland's competitiveness requires strong investment in basic research at universities. These are also the building blocks of sustainable economic growth. Investment in research and innovation is decided in Parliament, and that is one of many reasons why I am running in the parliamentary election.

My guiding principles

  • Nature's carrying capacity must not be exceeded.
  • The limits of what people can bear must not be exceeded.
  • The long-term effects of decisions must be taken into account.
  • No bill may be passed on to future generations.

Erkki Pulliainen's principles

I have chosen as my political guiding principles the theses of Erkki Pulliainen, a professor of zoology and long-serving Green member of the Finnish Parliament. He too was both a researcher and a politician.

None of these fit on the left–right axis, which is why they are easily sidelined in a debate divided along it. An example from my own field, soil science: most of Finland's farmland is clay soil. Ground frost keeps it workable, because freezing and thawing loosens the soil. If winters stop freezing the ground, clay soils will compact and harvests will shrink. There are plans for this, but they have been overshadowed by the rest of the political debate. If the question is left unresolved, future generations will have less arable land.

There is a lot of talk about the economy and government debt right now, and the concern is justified. But climate change and biodiversity loss are economic questions too. According to the Ministry of Finance (in Finnish), their overall economic impacts cannot currently be assessed accurately enough. So the economic debate is missing key risks, even though researchers could offer knowledge and figures on them. A small government debt is little comfort if future generations face many times larger bills for climate change and biodiversity loss. Curbing these threats remains the top priority, but we must also adapt. Adapting in advance costs less than repairing the damage.

Why the Greens

Because future generations should not pay for our mistakes.

I have always loved nature and animals, and the Greens were a considered choice for me. The Greens' values are my values. I bring the party expertise in soil and agriculture, which is needed as food production adapts to a changing climate.

The Greens fit the economy within nature's carrying capacity and see the market economy and entrepreneurship as part of the solution. Markets are efficient, but they need direction. When the harm done to nature is priced correctly, nature and the economy are not opposites. I do not want to support activities that stay profitable only because others pay for the harm they cause.

Every person is of equal worth, regardless of who they are or whom they love. I believe in liberal democracy and do not want a world where the state decides what is enough for each of us.

My goal is a democratic and free Finland with a sustainable way of life. Because future generations should not pay for our mistakes.

Politics · Positions

Where I stand

Here are my positions on a range of issues. My reasoning follows from my guiding principles: we must not exceed the carrying capacity of nature or the limits of human endurance, and we must not leave the bills to future generations.

Competitiveness through research and innovation

Finland's competitiveness comes from turning know-how, raw materials and data into valuable products here.

Research and development create new products, companies and jobs. They are also where we find the green transition solutions the world wants to buy. That is why investing in research is economic policy.

Our forests are turned into short-lived products, and the land use sector has gone from a carbon sink to a source of emissions. A higher degree of processing would get more value out of wood, for example: long-lived products such as wooden buildings and furniture, and materials that replace concrete, steel and plastic.

Data centres are an opportunity if the benefits stay in Finland: tax revenue, lasting jobs, expertise and its development, and waste heat put to use. Regulation must be strong and forward-looking so that Finland does not end up with nothing but empty halls. In the Sipoo council I submitted a motion on the data centre project.

What I will push forI will push to keep the planned increase in research and development funding, to study how wood could replace fossil-based and emission-intensive materials, and to assess data centre projects not only for their environmental impact but also for how much added value they leave in Finland.

Biodiversity also protects health

Protecting biodiversity also helps fight infectious diseases and antibiotic resistance.

Many dangerous epidemics began with environmental change caused by humans. In Malaysia in the late 1990s, deforestation drove fruit bats to pig farms, and the Nipah virus they spread killed more than a hundred people. Something similar is unlikely in Finland, but when forests are felled and green corridors are cut, deer that spread ticks and people meet more and more often.

Antibiotic resistance is an ancient, natural phenomenon. Bacteria have competed with one another using antibiotics for billions of years, and resistance genes are found everywhere in nature. In a diverse microbial community, resistant bacteria do not easily become more common. When diversity declines, room opens up for them. That is why biodiversity can act as a buffer against antibiotic resistance.

What I will push forIn Sipoo I submitted a council motion for a biodiversity action programme. In Parliament I will push for biodiversity to be taken into account as part of fighting infectious diseases and antibiotic resistance.

Security of supply starts in the field

Food security improves when farms produce energy themselves and replace mineral fertilisers with recycled fertilisers.

Finnish agriculture is too dependent on imported energy and on fertilisers made with fossil fuels. Russia's war of aggression has shown how vulnerable this dependence is. Energy tax refunds ease the strain on farms for a while, but they keep the same dependence in place.

Agricultural waste and side streams can be turned into biogas, and research shows that recycled fertilisers can safely replace mineral fertilisers. At the same time, nutrients that build up on livestock farms would reach crop farms, and new business would emerge in the countryside.

What I will push forI will push for agricultural crisis support to be directed at farms' own renewable energy, such as biogas, and at the use of recycled fertilisers.

Public services for people, not for systems

Public sector efficiency should be measured by how well services work, not by the number of staff.

Efficiency means producing the services people need with as little input as possible. As a user of health services, I often feel that today the system is being treated rather than the patient. When a care pathway goes wrong at the very start, costs grow later on. A service that works is also cheaper in the long run.

What I will push forI will push for services to be designed from the user's point of view, and for a study of how assessing the need for care over the phone affects the quality of care and total costs.

Basic income

Basic income suits a working life where more and more people earn their living from several sources. The current system makes people passive and discourages initiative.

An example from academia: the same person may run a business, write a doctoral thesis, receive grants and work part-time jobs now and then. If their income still isn't enough to live on, having several sources of income makes it harder to get support. The current system starts from suspicion of the applicant's honesty and demands proof of unemployment again and again. This forces people into passivity and breeds hopelessness. Basic income would dismantle the income traps and encourage people to take up work or try entrepreneurship.

What I will push forI support the Greens' basic income model (in Finnish). Until it is in place, I would make it easier to combine benefits with income from work, self-employment and grants.

Subsidies that harm the environment and our shared health

Tax money should not support activities whose harm is paid for by someone else.

Finland pays billions of euros in subsidies that harm nature and the environment. The tax exemption for wood fuels alone will be worth 470 million euros next year, more than three times the entire budget for environmental and nature protection. Subsidies like these distort markets and pass the bill on to future generations.

An example from agriculture: part of the support for milk production is paid according to output, so it pays for a farm to increase the number of animals. More animals mean more manure. Depending on the nutrient status of the fields, not all the manure can necessarily be spread on the fields best suited to it. Fields poorly suited to spreading, such as peatlands, then have to be used as well. Peat fields compact easily, and if the water table is not kept high enough, they release large amounts of greenhouse gases and leak nutrients into waterways.

Given Finnish conditions, I consider a certain amount of milk production sensible, if only for security of supply. But support for it must be designed so that it does not encourage farms to keep more animals than their fields can sustain. Before milk production, I would aim to reduce pork and broiler chicken production, as they carry a higher risk of animal diseases and diseases that spread to humans, as well as serious animal welfare problems.

Support for fur farming must end, and fur farming must be banned with a reasonable transition period. I would not subsidise the price of fuel at all. If support is needed, I would direct it straight to people on low incomes who are struggling, for example those with a long commute. They could then decide for themselves whether to spend it on fuel or, say, a bus ticket.

What I will push forI will push for harmful subsidies to be gradually redirected. In agriculture, support should be tied to product quality instead of output, with quality defined to take account of the effects on our shared health and the environment. I also support processing manure into recycled fertiliser so that it becomes a source of income for dairy farms as well.

Research

I study microbes. And manure.

I am a docent in microbiology and lead a research group at the University of Helsinki. I study how human activity changes the microbes in our environment and what that means for the shared health of people, animals and the environment.

Links between environmental diversity and microbial threats

What I study

Is a diverse microbial community a natural defence against antibiotic resistance, and have changes in land use taken that defence away?

Rising antibiotic resistance in bacteria is seen as a consequence of antibiotic use. That is true, but it is not the whole story. We humans did not invent antibiotics; we discovered them. Antibiotics are organic molecules produced by bacteria and other microbes that weaken bacteria. Antibiotic resistance is an ancient natural phenomenon rooted in billions of years of competition between bacteria for resources. Since the earliest days of life on Earth, bacteria have produced antibiotics and evolved resistance to them.

A diverse, undisturbed microbial community holds many species and many different microbe-made antibiotics in low concentrations. Such a community appears to keep resistance in check. Farming, settlement and industrialisation have impoverished the microbes in soil and the wider environment, freeing up room for specialised bacteria. I am investigating whether the loss of diversity, alongside antibiotic use, has laid the groundwork for today's antibiotic resistance crisis.

Our research group's method could be called microbial archaeology. We study ancient DNA and bacteria that have survived in a dormant state for hundreds or thousands of years, and compare microbial communities from different eras. Preliminary results show that relatives of today's clinically important resistance genes were already enriched in the waste and faecal deposits of 18th-century Turku, long before the antibiotic era. The results have been published as a preprint ahead of peer review, and the manuscript is currently under review at a scientific journal.

Our group's hypothesis is that nutrient loading in the environment and human activities that erode microbial diversity create room for microbes that harm people and other animals, including resistant bacteria. If our hypothesis holds, protecting biodiversity and restoring ecosystems could be one way to fight antibiotic resistance alongside the responsible use of antibiotics. They could also help prevent many microbial threats in agriculture.

Other lines of research

Resistance in agriculture and waste

Manure heaps, composts and landfills appear to be key places where antibiotic resistance becomes enriched. There, faecal bacteria meet antibiotic-producing environmental bacteria in nutrient-rich material. Studying these sites matters now that the use of recycled fertilisers is growing. In the EU's LEX4BIO project I led the work package that studied the risks of recycled fertilisers.

New antibiotics from ancient microbes

Many of the bacteria we have revived from archaeological samples inhibit the growth of other bacteria. They may produce compounds that today's pathogens have not adapted to. This research is new territory for our group.

Land use and waterways

Our study in Indonesia showed that land use shapes the microbes and resistance genes in river water.

Biosecurity on livestock farms

On Finnish pig farms, biosecurity was linked to the occurrence of resistant E. coli bacteria. This suggests resistance could be fought with the same measures as infectious animal diseases.

My career began at vocational school. I qualified as a laboratory technician in 2002 and worked for many years as a technician at the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine. Seeing up close what research really involves sparked my interest in science. I applied to university and graduated with a master's degree in soil and environmental science.

Because I wanted to become a researcher, I had to do a PhD. I found my topic at a lecture, when the lecturer remarked that nobody knows what happens to antibiotic residues in field soil, where they end up with manure from medicated animals. That is how microbiology became the field of my doctoral thesis, and I was able to start once I had secured funding.

My doctoral work showed that antibiotic residues are not a problem in Finnish agriculture, as their concentrations fall below the detection limit while the manure is still in storage. I did, however, find antibiotic resistance genes in bacteria, and they seemed to become enriched in stored manure. Resistance genes also turned up in unfertilised field soil, even genes against antibiotics that have never been used for animals in Finland, and these genes were not found in the manure.

I decided then that I wanted to find out what was going on, and that decision led me to where I am today. It is still not entirely clear what role resistance in environmental bacteria plays in today's antibiotic resistance crisis, but we keep learning more.

Expert roles

I serve as an expert in the newly established microbiome working group of the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA). My expertise has also been sought by the FAO and WHO, the Nordic Council of Ministers, the EU's Joint Research Centre, and Finland's Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry, Ministry of the Environment and Ministry of Social Affairs and Health. I have been chair or vice-chair of One Health Finland since 2016.

I have taught at the Universities of Helsinki and Turku and at Häme University of Applied Sciences, and supervised many students in Finland, the United States and Benin. I have talked about my research at events such as Science Night and on Finnish public broadcaster Yle's news.
